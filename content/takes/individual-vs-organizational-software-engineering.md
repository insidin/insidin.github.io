AI is making that distinction more important than ever. AI can generate working applications, complete features and even entire projects in minutes. Impressive results, often presented as evidence of how fundamentally software engineering is changing. But producing software is not the same as engineering software for an organization.

When I build something for myself, I can make compromises. I understand the context, accept the risks and may never need to maintain or extend the result. The software only needs to serve my purpose. But in an enterprise, software becomes an organizational responsibility. It needs to fit into a broader architecture, remain secure and maintainable, integrate with other systems and evolve as business needs change. People who weren't involved in creating it must be able to operate, understand and take responsibility for it. The real difference isn't the complexity of the software. It's the organization's ability to own and evolve it over time.

This is where I believe much of today's AI productivity debate misses the point. We're measuring how quickly AI can produce software, while paying far less attention to how effectively organizations can absorb, govern, operate and evolve what gets produced. And as AI dramatically reduces the effort required to create software, that distinction becomes even more critical. Generating software is becoming cheaper. Owning software isn't necessarily becoming easier.

The implications go well beyond development teams. They affect enterprise architecture, IT strategy, operating models, governance and ultimately the amount of complexity an organization can sustainably manage. We need to rethink how we measure engineering productivity and value, not simply celebrate how much more code we can generate. The real measure of AI-powered software engineering isn't how much software we can produce. It's how much software our organizations can responsibly own.

We've traditionally governed processes and software through permissions, predefined behavior and approval steps. We define what a human or a system can access, what actions can be performed and what controls apply. But autonomous AI challenges a fundamental assumption behind that approach: can we reasonably anticipate how an AI system will behave?

In recent security incidents reported by OpenAI and Anthropic, during cybersecurity evaluations, AI agents found ways around technical boundaries, exploited vulnerabilities and accessed systems they weren't authorized to access. These were controlled evaluations, not ordinary enterprise deployments, but they reveal a problem that goes far beyond cybersecurity. An AI agent doesn't have to pursue the wrong objective to become dangerous: it can pursue the right objective in entirely unacceptable ways.

Imagine an enterprise AI agent tasked with reducing operational costs, improving debt collection or increasing customer retention. We give it access to systems, define KPIs and expect it to optimize the outcome. But what if the most effective path involves manipulating customers, exploiting weaknesses in other systems or making decisions nobody explicitly authorized?

Our existing governance models tend to focus on approving the objective, assessing the solution and controlling access to resources. With autonomous agents, that may no longer be sufficient. We need to govern not just the objective, but the boundaries within which agents are allowed to pursue it. That requires connecting business intent, risk appetite and delegated authority to executable policies, contextual authorization and continuous verification. Not to prescribe every step an agent takes, but to make explicit which means are unacceptable, regardless of how effectively they achieve the desired outcome.

And perhaps this is the bigger organizational challenge: we've spent decades managing performance through objectives and KPIs, relying on human judgment to fill the gaps. AI may force us to discover just how much of that judgment was never captured in our governance models.
